Phone storage full? How to free up space safely

The order matters more than the technique. Back up, verify, then delete. This guide covers what to do when phone storage is full, how to find the files actually taking the room, and how to move photos off phone storage without losing anything you meant to keep.

14 min read Updated September 2026 Android and iPhone Beginner friendly

A phone storage full notification almost always arrives at the worst possible moment, usually while you are trying to photograph something that will not wait. The instinct is to start deleting immediately. That instinct is what causes people to lose photos permanently.

There is a safe order for this, and it takes about two minutes longer than the panic version. Back up first, confirm the backup is real, then delete local copies. Doing it in that order means everything you remove is a duplicate rather than an original, and nothing you clear is actually gone.

This guide covers how to free up phone storage on both Android and iPhone, how to work out what is genuinely using the space, why video is nearly always the answer even when it does not feel like it, and where to store phone photos so the problem does not simply return in six weeks.

Do this before you delete anything

If you read one section, make it this one. Everything else on this page is detail, and this is the part that decides whether you free up space or lose files.

When your phone says storage is full, the photos and videos on it exist in exactly one place. Deleting them is permanent. There is no service, app or repair shop that can retrieve a photo that was never copied anywhere else, and the recently deleted folder only buys you 30 days.

So the sequence is always the same, regardless of which phone you have:

  • Back up everything first and wait until it genuinely finishes, not until it looks like it has started working.
  • Confirm the files arrived by opening several of them from inside your account, ideally in a browser on a different device.
  • Delete local copies in small batches, checking as you go.
  • Empty the recently deleted folder, which is the step people forget and the reason deleting often frees no space at all.

Never delete local files while a backup is still running. If a file has not finished uploading and you remove the original, there is nothing left for the upload to finish, and the file is gone from both places.

If you do not have a backup yet, set one up before clearing anything. It takes a few minutes and it turns this from a risky cleanup into a routine one. The full backup setup guide walks through it on any platform.

First, work out which storage is actually full

This trips up a surprising number of people, and it leads to buying more of the wrong thing.

Device storage is the physical space inside the phone. This is what stops you taking photos, installing apps or recording video. It is the one this guide is mostly about.

Account or cloud storage is the space in whatever online service you use. When that fills, uploads pause, but your phone carries on working normally.

The two produce similar looking warnings and need completely different responses. People regularly pay for more cloud storage while their device is the thing that is full, or clear device space while their uploads have been paused for months. Read the notification carefully before acting on it.

To check device storage, the path is short on both platforms:

  • iPhone: Settings, then General, then iPhone Storage.
  • Android: Settings, then Storage. Some manufacturers put it under Device care or Battery and device care.

Both give you a breakdown sorted by what is using the most, which is where the next section starts.

Find what is taking the space, biggest first

Sort by file size rather than working through your gallery chronologically. This single change is the difference between ten minutes of work and an entire evening of it.

The storage breakdown on both platforms will almost always show Photos at or near the top. Within Photos, the split between stills and video is where the real story is, and it is rarely what people expect.

Those numbers explain almost every case of a phone filling up unexpectedly. Ten minutes of 4K footage uses roughly the same room as a thousand photographs. If your gallery looks modest but your storage keeps vanishing, video is the reason, essentially always.

This is why photos taking too much storage is the common complaint while videos taking too much phone storage is usually the actual cause. Archiving five or six long clips frequently frees more room than deleting several hundred photos, and it takes a fraction of the time and none of the emotional effort.

Start with your longest videos. It is the highest return for the least work, and you are far less likely to regret archiving a twenty minute clip you have never rewatched than a photo of someone.

The other things quietly using space

Photos and video are usually the bulk, but they are not the whole picture. A few others are worth a look once the obvious wins are done:

  • App caches. Streaming and social apps hold downloaded content and thumbnails, sometimes several gigabytes of it. Clearing a cache is safe and does not sign you out or remove anything you saved.
  • Downloaded media. Offline episodes, podcasts and maps you saved for a trip two years ago.
  • Messaging attachments. Every photo and video anyone has ever sent you, stored in full, often duplicated from the conversation.
  • Old app installers and files. On Android, the Downloads folder collects things indefinitely and nothing ever clears it for you.
  • The recently deleted folder. Covered below, because it deserves its own explanation.

How to free up phone storage without losing anything

Once your backup is confirmed, the copies on the phone are duplicates. Removing them frees space and costs you nothing, because the copy in your account remains exactly where it is. The care is all in the verification, not in the deleting.

A safe sequence, in order:

  • Confirm the backup status reads complete, not in progress, paused or waiting for Wi-Fi.
  • Open several files from your account in a browser on another device. Check your oldest and your most recent, not just one convenient example.
  • Delete a small batch locally first, perhaps a single month, then confirm those files still open from your account afterwards.
  • Work through the rest once that has proved itself, largest files first.
  • Empty the recently deleted folder to actually reclaim the space.

Opening a photo from your phone gallery proves nothing, because the phone has its own copy either way. Opening it in a browser on a laptop is the check that means something, and it is the step almost everyone skips.

Why deleting often frees no space at all

Both platforms keep deleted photos in a holding folder for up to 30 days so you can undo a mistake. It is a genuinely good feature, and it means a deletion does not free any room until that folder is emptied.

People delete several hundred photos, watch the storage figure refuse to move, conclude something is broken, and delete more. On iPhone this is Recently Deleted inside the Photos app. On Android it is usually Bin or Trash in your gallery app, and Google Photos keeps its own separate one.

Empty the recently deleted folder last, after you have confirmed everything else is fine. Until you do, those files are still recoverable, which is a useful safety net while you are partway through a cleanup.

How to free up storage on Android

Android gives you more direct access to the file system than iOS, which makes cleanup easier once you know where to look.

Settings, then Storage, gives you a breakdown by category. Tapping into Photos and videos or Media lets you sort by size, which is where you should start. Most Android gallery apps also have a sort option that is set to date by default and worth switching to size for this job.

Three things specific to Android are worth checking:

  • The Downloads folder. Nothing ever clears this. It collects PDFs, images saved from browsers, and installers, sometimes for years.
  • Messaging app media folders. These sit separately from your camera folder and often hold more than the camera folder does.
  • The gallery bin. Your gallery app and Google Photos may each keep their own, so emptying one does not empty the other.

If you are on a Samsung, Device care includes a storage cleanup tool that identifies duplicates and large files for you. It is reasonable at finding candidates, but it should not be the thing deciding what gets deleted. Confirm your backup first regardless of what any cleanup tool suggests.

How to free up iPhone storage

Settings, then General, then iPhone Storage gives you a list sorted largest first, along with a set of recommendations at the top. Those recommendations are worth reading and worth being slightly careful with.

The useful ones are straightforward: reviewing large attachments in Messages, and offloading apps you have not opened in a year, which removes the app but keeps its data so reinstalling puts everything back.

Two iPhone specific things to understand before you start deleting:

  • Recently Deleted holds items for 30 days. Photos, then Albums, then scroll to Recently Deleted, then delete all. Without this step the space does not come back.
  • If iCloud Photos is on, deleting syncs everywhere. Removing a photo from your iPhone removes it from iCloud and from your iPad too, because that is what syncing does. A separate backup is unaffected, which is precisely the reason to have one.

That second point is the one that catches people out, and it is worth being clear about. iCloud Photos is a sync service rather than a backup. It keeps the same library on all your Apple devices, faithfully including the deletions. If your only copy is an iCloud copy, a cleanup on the phone is a cleanup everywhere.

How to store large videos so they stop filling the phone

Video is what fills a phone, and it does it faster than almost anyone expects. Understanding the numbers is what stops the problem returning.

A minute of 1080p costs roughly 60MB to 100MB. A minute of 4K can reach 350MB or more. Frame rate matters as much as resolution: 4K at 60 frames per second is roughly double the size of 4K at 30, for footage that looks near identical on a phone screen.

Plenty of phones ship with the higher setting enabled by default, which means a lot of people are recording at twice the file size without ever having chosen to. Checking that one setting is the highest value thing you can do for future storage.

  • iPhone: Settings, then Camera, then Record Video.
  • Android: open the camera app, then its settings, then video resolution.

Dropping everyday clips to 1080p and reserving 4K for footage you actually intend to keep or edit reduces video file sizes to roughly a quarter. That alone can multiply how long your phone lasts between cleanups.

For the clips you already have, cloud storage for large files works the same as anything else. Upload them, confirm they arrived, then remove the local copies. There is no separate mechanism for big files, they simply take longer to move.

Where to store phone photos so this does not happen again

Clearing space is a one off. Not needing to clear it again is the actual goal, and that depends on where the photos go instead.

There are three realistic options and they fail in different ways, which is worth knowing before choosing one.

An SD card

Cheap, and right there in the phone. It is also the option most likely to lose your photos. Cards fail without warning, and because the card lives in the same phone as the original, a lost or damaged handset takes both copies at once. A card is extra room, not a backup.

A computer

This genuinely works and for a long time it was the standard answer. The problem is that it depends on you remembering. In practice that happens for a few months and then stops. It also puts everything on one drive in one house, which is a single point of failure if that drive dies.

Online cloud storage

This removes the remembering. Once set up, new photos upload without anyone deciding to do it, and the copy sits somewhere physically separate from the phone. That is what makes it a backup rather than a second pile of files, and it is why moving photos off phone storage stops being a recurring chore.

The strongest arrangement is not one of the three, it is two. Cloud for the automatic everyday copy, plus an occasional export to a drive for anything genuinely irreplaceable. One copy that needs no attention, one that does not depend on any account at all.

Asked plainly, how to get more phone storage without buying a new handset comes down to this: stop treating the phone as the place your photos live. It is the device you capture them on. Once a cloud backup app is handling the copy, how to save phone storage every month afterwards stops being a task you have to think about at all.

If you only do one, make it the automatic one. A system that runs without you beats a perfect manual routine that nobody keeps up.

How much storage do you need to stop worrying about it

Storage figures are meaningless until you translate them into things you recognise. A gigabyte holds roughly 250 to 400 phone photos, which puts 100GB somewhere between 25,000 and 40,000 images.

You can work out your own rate in about a minute. Look at what Photos is using in your storage settings and divide by how old the phone is. A two year old phone with 40GB of photos is generating roughly 20GB a year, which means 100GB represents about five years of headroom from a standing start.

For most people who shoot photos and the occasional short clip, 100GB free cloud storage is years rather than months of headroom. If you record long 4K video routinely, expect it to move considerably faster and plan on archiving finished footage rather than keeping everything live.

Common problems and how to fix them

I deleted hundreds of photos and nothing changed. The recently deleted folder is holding them for 30 days. Empty it and the space returns immediately.

Storage says full but my gallery looks small. Video, almost certainly. Sort by size rather than date and check the longest clips first.

I cleared space and it filled again within weeks. Recording resolution is the usual cause. Check whether you are shooting 4K at 60fps by default rather than by choice.

I deleted a photo from my phone and it vanished from my iPad. That is sync behaviour, not a fault. A separate backup is unaffected, which is the reason to keep one alongside sync.

My uploads have paused and my phone is still full. Two different storages. Your account is full rather than the device, so clearing the phone will not resume uploads. Free space in the account or move to a larger plan.

The phone is too full to even install a backup app. Clear an app cache or delete a couple of long videos you have already shared elsewhere. That buys enough room to install, back up properly, then clear the rest safely.

Frequently asked questions

The questions that come up most often about a full phone, freeing up space and where photos should go instead.

What should I do first when my phone storage is full?
Back up before you delete anything. Once a backup is confirmed, the files on your phone are duplicates and removing them costs you nothing. Deleting first is how people lose photos permanently, because a photo that exists in only one place cannot be recovered once it is gone.
Why did deleting photos not free up any space?
Deleted photos sit in a recently deleted folder for up to 30 days before the space is released. On iPhone it is Recently Deleted in the Photos app. On Android it is usually Bin or Trash in your gallery, and Google Photos keeps a separate one. Empty it and the space returns.
Do photos or videos take up more phone storage?
Video, by a wide margin. A photo is roughly 2MB to 5MB while a minute of 4K video can reach 350MB or more. Ten minutes of 4K uses about the same room as a thousand photographs, which is why archiving a few long clips frees more space than deleting hundreds of pictures.
Does backing up to the cloud delete photos from my phone?
No. Backing up creates a copy and leaves your originals exactly where they are. Your gallery looks identical afterwards. Removing local copies to reclaim space is a separate action you choose to take later, once you have confirmed the backup worked.
How do I move photos off my phone without losing them?
Upload them, verify they arrived by opening several from your account in a browser on another device, then delete the local copies in small batches. Checking from the phone itself proves nothing, since the phone has its own copy regardless of whether the upload succeeded.
Is an SD card a good place to store phone photos?
It is extra room rather than a backup. Cards fail without warning, and because the card is inside the same phone as the original, a lost or damaged handset takes both copies at once. It is fine as additional space, provided a second copy exists somewhere else.
How much cloud storage do I need to clear my phone?
Check what Photos is using in your storage settings, since that figure is everything you would be moving. For most people it is well under 100GB, which is what the free plan includes. If you record a lot of 4K video, 250GB, 500GB and 1TB plans are available.
Why does my phone fill up again so quickly after clearing it?
Usually recording resolution. Many phones ship set to 4K at 60 frames per second, which is roughly eight times the file size of 1080p at 30 for footage that looks near identical on a phone screen. Changing that one setting slows the refill dramatically.

The whole thing as a checklist

Everything above, in the order you should actually do it.

  • Check whether it is your device or your account that is full
  • Back up everything and wait for it to genuinely finish
  • Open several files from your account in a browser to confirm they are there
  • Sort your storage by file size rather than by date
  • Delete a small test batch locally, then confirm those files still open from your account
  • Work through the rest, longest videos first
  • Clear app caches and old downloads for a second round of easy wins
  • Empty the recently deleted folder to actually reclaim the space
  • Drop your recording resolution so the problem takes years to return rather than weeks

The verification step is the one worth protecting. A backup you have never opened from somewhere other than the phone is an assumption rather than a safety net, and a cleanup is exactly the moment that distinction becomes expensive.

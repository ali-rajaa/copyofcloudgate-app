What online cloud storage does

Backing up means keeping a second copy of your files somewhere other than your phone. That is the whole idea, and almost every problem people have with cloud storage comes from misunderstanding this one point.

When you back up a photo, the photo does not leave your phone. A copy of it travels to your online storage account and sits there alongside everything else you have uploaded. Your gallery looks exactly the same afterwards. Nothing is moved, nothing is hidden, and nothing is deleted.

This matters because a large number of people avoid setting up a cloud backup app at all, having assumed their photos will vanish into the internet and only exist online from then on. They will not. The local file stays where it is until you personally decide to delete it, which some people do later on purpose to free up phone storage once the backup is confirmed.

Any decent cloud storage app works the same way, whether you think of it as online file storage, file storage online, a free storage app, a file backup app or personal cloud storage. Mobile cloud storage and desktop access are the same account seen from different screens. The label changes, the principle does not: one copy on the device, one copy in your account.

The useful consequence is that your files stop being tied to one piece of hardware. Once a photo is in online cloud storage you can open it on a laptop that has never touched your phone, put it back on a different handset, or reach it from a borrowed computer if your phone is lost. A lost phone becomes an inconvenience rather than a loss.

Backing up protects your files. Freeing up space is a separate action you take afterwards, once you have checked the backup worked.

You do not need to delete anything first, you do not need to sort your photos, and you do not need to know how much storage you are using. The app handles the ordering. Trying to tidy up before backing up is a common reason people start this process and never finish it.

You are also not committing to much. Setting up cloud storage does not lock your files into one service, because you can download everything back out at any point from the app or a browser. Nothing here is difficult to undo, which is a reasonable thing to want reassurance about before handing several years of photos to something new.

Step 2: Turn on automatic backup

Open the app and find the backup settings. You are looking for one main switch, usually described as automatic backup or auto upload. Turn it on.

The important word is automatic. Manual backup works perfectly for about two weeks, which is roughly how long anyone remembers to do it. The entire point of a cloud backup app is that it runs on the days you are not thinking about it, including the day your phone ends up in a sink.

When you enable it, the app asks for permission to reach your photos and files. Grant full access rather than limited or selected access.

Choosing limited or selected photo access is one of the most common reasons a backup looks like it is running while only ever capturing a handful of files. If you are unsure which you picked, check now rather than finding out later.

On Android you may also see a prompt about allowing the app to run in the background. Say yes. Without it, the system can pause uploads whenever the screen is off, which is precisely when you want them happening.

Step 3: Choose which folders get backed up

This step decides whether your backup covers you, and most guides skip past it in a sentence.

Most backup tools default to your main camera folder. That captures photos and videos you took yourself, and misses a surprising share of everything else on the phone. Go through the folder list and make sure these are included:

  • Camera, everything you photographed or filmed
  • Screenshots, which hold booking confirmations, tickets, receipts and reference information
  • Downloads, where PDFs, statements and email attachments land
  • Messaging media, photos and videos other people sent you
  • Documents, if you scan paperwork or save files directly to the phone

Messaging media is the folder people forget most often, and it is frequently the one holding photos of family and friends that exist nowhere else. Those images were never in your camera roll, so a camera only backup will never touch them. If you have ever lost a phone and later realised the group photos everyone sent you are gone, this is why.

This is also the step that decides whether you have free photo cloud storage covering everything, or free video cloud storage covering only what the camera app produced. A free storage app Android owners install will default to the narrowest folder set unless told otherwise. Folder selection usually decides whether a backup is complete, more than plan size does.

Step 4: Decide between Wi-Fi only and mobile data

You will see an option for uploading over Wi-Fi only. It is switched on by default, and it should usually stay that way. There is one important exception.

If you are rarely connected to Wi-Fi, perhaps because you mostly use mobile data or you are travelling, Wi-Fi only means your backup may not run for weeks. It will look enabled the whole time and do nothing.

If your last backup date is older than you expect, check this setting first. A Wi-Fi only backup on a phone that never sees Wi-Fi is the most common cause of a stalled backup by a wide margin.

A sensible middle ground is allowing photos over mobile data while keeping video restricted to Wi-Fi. Photos are small enough that a day of them barely registers against a typical allowance. Video is the item worth holding back, since a few minutes of 4K footage can use as much data as several hundred photographs.

Step 5: Confirm the first backup actually finished

This step is the one people skip, and it determines whether the previous four did anything.

Your first backup is much larger than any that follow, because it is uploading your entire library rather than today's handful of files. Depending on how many years of photos you have and your connection speed, it can take anywhere from several minutes to overnight.

Leave the phone charging and connected to Wi-Fi, then come back later and check three things:

  1. The status reads complete or up to date, rather than in progress or paused
  2. The file count roughly matches what you would expect from your gallery
  3. You can open a recent photo from inside your account, not only from your phone gallery

That third check is the one that counts. Watching a photo load from your online storage is proof the file made it. A status label is just a label.

If you are on Android

Android has three settings that stop a backup, and all three are set against you by default: photo access limited to selected items rather than all of them, battery optimisation suspending the app after a fortnight, and on Samsung handsets a second power manager in Device Care that sleeps apps independently of the first.

Folder selection matters more on Android too, because messaging media, screenshots and downloads each live in their own folder and a default backup usually covers only the camera folder.

Phone storage full? How to free up phone storage

A full phone is often the reason to go looking for cloud storage. Once a backup exists, freeing up phone storage is quick and safe.

The order matters, and getting it wrong is how people lose things:

  1. Back up everything first and wait for it to finish
  2. Confirm the files are in your account by opening a few of them there
  3. Only then delete the local copies from your phone

Never delete local files before you have opened them from your account. A status label saying complete is not the same as seeing the file load.

How to move photos off phone, biggest files first

Sort your storage by file size rather than working through your gallery chronologically. It is almost always long videos sitting at the top. Archiving five or six of them to cloud storage and removing the local copies usually frees more space than deleting several hundred photos, and you lose nothing either way because the originals remain in your account.

Do not forget the other space users

Photos and videos are usually the bulk, but cached data from streaming and social apps can hold several gigabytes. Clearing an app cache is safe and does not sign you out or remove anything you saved.

If photos taking too much storage is the immediate complaint, and videos taking too much phone storage is the underlying cause, the fix is the same either way. Decide where to store phone photos permanently, upload them, and only then reclaim the space. That is how to get more phone storage without deleting anything you care about, and how to save phone storage every month afterwards.

What to do when phone storage is full comes down to this: back up first, verify, then delete locally. Knowing how to store large videos separately is the other half, because a handful of long clips is almost always what filled the device.

Where to backup photos: how to store photos online

Online photo storage answers a question people phrase in a dozen ways. Where to back up photos, how to save phone photos online, how to store photos outside the phone. They are all the same problem: photos that exist in exactly one place.

A phone is a single point of failure. It gets lost, stolen, dropped, soaked, or stops working one morning with no warning. Anything that exists only on that device disappears with it, and no service can retrieve a photo that was never uploaded anywhere.

Keep originals rather than compressed copies

Some services shrink your photos to save room on their end. That is fine for a quick look on a phone screen and disappointing if you ever want to print, crop or edit later. Storing full quality originals means the file you get back is the file you took.

This is worth weighing when comparing options, because the best way to store photos is whichever one hands back exactly what you put in. Online photo backup that reduces quality is fine for convenience and poor for anything you plan to keep. If you only remember one thing about how to backup photos properly, make it that the originals should come back unchanged.

Cloud storage for videos and large video files

Video is the file type that breaks people's mental model of storage, because the numbers are so different from photos.

A minute of 1080p footage uses roughly 60MB to 100MB. The same minute recorded in 4K can reach 350MB or more. Ten minutes of 4K therefore uses about as much room as a thousand photographs. If your library looks small but your storage keeps disappearing, video is nearly always the explanation.

Keep video uploads on Wi-Fi only even if you allow photos over mobile data. This one setting prevents the vast majority of unexpected data usage, while still keeping your everyday photos backed up as you take them.

How much cloud storage do you need

Storage figures mean nothing until you translate them into things you recognise. These are realistic numbers for a current phone.

  • One photo: roughly 2MB to 5MB, so 1GB holds about 250 to 400 photos
  • 100GB: somewhere between 25,000 and 40,000 photos
  • One minute of 1080p video: around 60MB to 100MB
  • One minute of 4K video: 350MB or more
  • A scanned document: a few hundred kilobytes, effectively free

At a typical photo rate, 100GB of free cloud storage holds years of material. You will usually fill your phone's own storage several times over before you come close to filling an account that size.

That is the case for free 100GB cloud storage as a starting point: 100GB online storage free lasts years of photos, and free online file backup at that scale removes the usual reason for putting a backup off.

The exception is heavy video. If you regularly record long 4K clips, or you keep footage from a camera as well as a phone, expect the maths to move faster and plan on archiving finished projects rather than keeping everything live forever.

Keeping backups running month after month

Setting up a backup is a one off. Keeping it running takes about five seconds a month, and skipping that is how people end up with a backup that stopped in March.

Check your last backup date

Open the app once a month and glance at when the last backup finished. If it is older than a few days, something has paused it. This single habit catches every problem below long before it matters.

Watch out after system updates

Major Android and iOS updates occasionally reset app permissions. A reset photo permission stops a backup instantly and silently, so check it after any large update.

Battery settings drift

Phones sometimes re-apply battery restrictions to apps you have not opened in a while, particularly on Android. If backups stop without an obvious cause, this is the first place to look.

Storage filling up

When an account is full, new uploads stop while everything already stored stays exactly where it is. Nothing is deleted. You either clear some room or move to a larger plan, and uploads resume.

Common problems and how to fix them

Nearly every backup complaint traces back to one of these five things. Each takes under a minute to check.

Backup says complete but files are missing. This is almost always folder selection rather than a failure. Go back to your folder list and check whether the missing files live somewhere you never enabled, particularly messaging media.

Backup has not run in weeks. Check Wi-Fi only settings first, then battery optimisation. Between them these explain the large majority of stalled backups.

Only a few photos uploaded. You almost certainly granted limited or selected photo access instead of full access. Change it in your phone's app permissions and the rest will follow.

Uploads stop when the screen turns off. Background activity is restricted. Allow background app refresh on iPhone, or set the app to unrestricted battery usage on Android.

Account is full. Nothing is lost, uploads pause. Clear space by removing large videos you no longer need, or move to a larger plan.

If a backup looks stuck partway through, it is usually waiting rather than broken. Connect to Wi-Fi, plug the phone in, leave the screen off for a while, and check again before assuming something failed.

How to move files from phone to cloud on a new device

New phone day is when a backup is most useful, and also when files most often get lost. The order of operations is everything.

  1. Run a final backup on the old phone and wait until it reports complete
  2. Open two or three recent files from inside your account to confirm they are there
  3. Sign in to your account on the new phone and let your library download
  4. Check contacts, messages and anything outside your cloud storage transferred separately
  5. Only then factory reset or trade in the old device

Once a factory reset finishes, anything that was not backed up is gone permanently. No app, service or repair shop can recover it. It is the most expensive mistake in this guide.

Because your files live in your account rather than a manufacturer's device backup, switching between Android and iPhone works in either direction. Sign in on the new phone and your library is available, regardless of what the old one was.

The whole thing as a checklist

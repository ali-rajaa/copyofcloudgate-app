Cloud storage for videos and large video files

Video breaks most people's mental model of storage. Real file sizes for 1080p and 4K, where to store large videos, and how to back them up without burning a data allowance.

12 min read Updated {{ page.last_modified_at | date: "%-d %B %Y" }} Android, iPhone and web Beginner friendly

Almost everyone underestimates video by an order of magnitude. People picture it as a photo that moves, so a few clips feel like a few pictures. In storage terms a single minute of 4K weighs as much as a hundred photographs.

That gap between expectation and reality is behind most storage surprises. A phone fills in weeks rather than years, a backup that ran comfortably for months suddenly stalls, or a month of mobile data disappears in an afternoon. Video is the explanation nearly every time.

This guide covers what video files actually weigh, where to store large videos so they stop crowding everything else, how to back up videos online without destroying a data allowance, and the one camera setting that halves your file sizes with no visible difference on a phone screen.

What video files actually weigh

These numbers are the whole guide. Everything else follows from them.

One minute of 1080p
350MB+
One minute of 4K

Put another way: ten minutes of 4K footage occupies roughly the same room as a thousand photographs. A single school concert filmed end to end can outweigh an entire year of casual photos.

Frame rate compounds it. Recording 4K at 60 frames per second produces files about double the size of 4K at 30, for footage that looks essentially identical on a phone screen. A great many phones ship with the higher setting switched on by default, so plenty of people record at twice the file size without ever having chosen to.

Some rough translations into storage you can picture:

  • 100GB holds around 16 to 25 hours of 1080p, or roughly 4 to 5 hours of 4K.
  • 250GB holds around 10 to 12 hours of 4K, which suits someone filming regularly.
  • 500GB covers heavy 4K recording or footage from a separate camera alongside phone video.
  • 1TB is family territory, several people filming into one account.

If your library looks small but your storage keeps disappearing, sort by file size rather than by date. The answer is almost always sitting in the top ten results, and it is almost always video.

Where to store large videos

Long recordings do not belong on a phone. They are the files most likely to fill it, least likely to be rewatched, and most painful to lose, which is an awkward combination.

The realistic options, and how each one fails:

Leave them on the phone

Works until it does not. The phone fills, and everything is in one place, so a lost or broken handset takes every clip with it. Video is also the category people most often delete under pressure when storage runs out, which is how footage disappears permanently.

An external drive

Genuinely good for finished projects and large archives, and cheap per gigabyte. The weakness is that it only protects you if you actually connect it, which happens enthusiastically for a while and then stops. It is also one drive in one building.

Online video storage

Upload them once, confirm they arrived, then clear the local copies. Cloud storage for videos means the file is reachable from any device without carrying anything, and the copy is physically separate from the phone that recorded it.

There is nothing special about big files here, despite how it feels. Cloud storage for large video files works exactly like anything else, it simply takes longer to move. The only real difference is that you want the first upload running on Wi-Fi with the phone on power.

Video cloud storage is not a separate product either. The same account that holds your photos will store videos in cloud form alongside them, and free cloud storage for videos is simply your ordinary allowance being used by larger files. When people ask how to backup videos online, the answer is the same process as anything else, run with a bit more patience.

The practical arrangement for most people: everything goes to cloud storage automatically, and anything genuinely irreplaceable gets an extra copy on a drive once a year. One copy that needs no effort, one that depends on no account.

Backing up videos without burning your data

This single setting prevents the large majority of unexpected mobile data bills, and it takes about fifteen seconds to check.

Keep video uploads restricted to Wi-Fi, even if you allow photos over mobile data. Photos are small enough that a day of them barely registers against a typical allowance. Video is the item worth holding back, because a few minutes of 4K can consume as much data as several hundred photographs.

That split is the sensible default for most people: photos upload as you take them so they are protected immediately, while video waits for a network that does not cost anything.

The trade-off is worth understanding. Video sitting in a queue waiting for Wi-Fi is not backed up yet. If you are travelling for three weeks without a network, that footage exists in exactly one place for three weeks. For an ordinary trip that is a reasonable risk. For footage you could never film again, connect to something and let it upload.

Never delete a local video while its upload is still queued or in progress. A file that has not finished transferring has nothing behind it, so removing the original loses it from both places at once.

Large uploads also need the phone to stay awake and connected. Plug it in, leave it on Wi-Fi, and let the screen go off rather than sitting and watching it. On Android, allowing background activity for the app matters more for video than for anything else, simply because the transfers take longer.

The recording setting that halves your file sizes

Before solving where to put the files, it is worth reducing how large they are in the first place. This is the highest value thing on this page for anyone whose storage keeps refilling.

  • iPhone: Settings, then Camera, then Record Video.
  • Android: open the camera app, then its settings, then video resolution.

Dropping everyday recording to 1080p at 30 frames per second cuts file sizes to roughly a quarter of 4K at 60. On a phone screen, and on most televisions at normal viewing distance, the difference is difficult to see. Reserve 4K for footage you intend to edit, project or keep permanently.

Two situations genuinely justify the larger setting. Filming something you will crop or stabilise later benefits from the extra resolution, and footage of a one-off event you will still be watching in twenty years is worth the room. Everyday clips of the dog are not.

Changing this does nothing to video you have already recorded. Those files stay the size they are, which is what the rest of this guide is about.

How to save videos without phone storage filling again

Once a video is uploaded and confirmed, the copy on the phone is a duplicate. Removing it costs nothing and frees a meaningful amount of room in a single action, which is far less tedious than picking through photos.

The safe order, which matters more with video than with anything else because the files are so large:

  • Upload and wait for it to genuinely finish, not just to start.
  • Play the video from your account in a browser on another device. Watch a few seconds from the middle, not just the opening frame.
  • Delete the local copy once it plays.
  • Empty the recently deleted folder, or the space will not actually return for 30 days.

Playing from a thumbnail proves nothing, because thumbnails generate from partial files. Watching a few seconds from the middle confirms the whole transfer completed. With a 4GB file that distinction matters, since a truncated upload can look complete in a list view.

A habit worth building: archive finished footage rather than keeping everything live. Events, holidays and projects that are done can live entirely in your account. Only recent material needs to be on the phone, and a phone treated as a camera rather than an archive stops filling up.

If your device is already out of room, the guide to freeing up space covers the safe order for clearing it.

How much storage video actually needs

Video is the one input that makes an allowance run out, so it is worth sizing against how much you film rather than how many photos you have.

A quick estimate: check what video is using in your phone's storage settings, divide by how long you have had the phone, and you have your annual rate. Someone at 40GB of video after two years is generating around 20GB a year. Someone who films 4K events regularly can pass that in a month.

Common problems and how to fix them

My photos backed up but my videos did not. Wi-Fi only is applied to video while photos are allowed on mobile data. The clips are queued rather than failed, and will move once you join a network.

A large video has been uploading for hours. Normal for multi-gigabyte files. Home connections upload far more slowly than they download. Leave it on power overnight before assuming a fault.

My account filled up much faster than expected. Check how much of it is video. A handful of long 4K clips can occupy more room than several years of photographs.

The upload restarts every time I open the app. Background activity is restricted, so transfers only progress while the app is on screen. Allow background refresh, and keep the phone on power for large files.

Videos people sent me are not backed up. Those sit in messaging app folders rather than your camera folder. Add messaging media to your folder selection.

I used a month of mobile data in a day. Video uploading over mobile data. Restrict video to Wi-Fi and leave photos on either, which is the setting most people want.

The whole thing as a checklist

  • Check your recording resolution and drop everyday clips to 1080p
  • Sort your storage by file size to find the clips actually using the room
  • Keep video uploads on Wi-Fi, photos on either
  • Start large uploads on power, on Wi-Fi, with the screen off
  • Add messaging media so videos people sent you are covered
  • Play a few seconds from the middle of an uploaded clip to confirm it transferred
  • Only then delete local copies, and empty the recently deleted folder
  • Archive finished footage rather than keeping everything on the phone

The resolution setting and the Wi-Fi setting between them solve most video storage problems before they start. Everything else on this page is cleanup for footage you already have.

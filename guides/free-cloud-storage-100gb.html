How many photos fit in 100GB

A photo from a current phone camera usually lands between 2MB and 5MB, depending on detail and lighting.

That puts one gigabyte at roughly 250 to 400 photos, and 100GB somewhere between 25,000 and 40,000 images. To make that concrete: if you take twenty photos a day, every day, you would need somewhere between three and five years to fill it with photographs alone.

Most people take considerably fewer than twenty a day averaged across a year. Free photo cloud storage at this size is, for a normal phone photographer, effectively a decade of headroom.

A few things push the number down. Portrait mode shots store additional depth data. RAW files, if your phone supports them and you have turned them on, are several times the size of a normal photo. Burst sequences produce a lot of frames very quickly. None of these change the order of magnitude, they just move you toward the lower end of the range.

Things that push it up: HEIC on iPhone and comparable formats on Android produce smaller files at similar quality, so a modern library is often more efficient than an older one at the same resolution.

The quick estimate: a gigabyte holds roughly 300 phone photos. Multiply by whatever allowance you are looking at and you have a usable figure.

How much video fits, and why it changes everything

Video is the item that rewrites the maths, and it does it by a factor most people find surprising.

A minute of 1080p footage costs around 60MB to 100MB. The same minute recorded in 4K can reach 350MB or more. Ten minutes of 4K therefore uses about as much room as a thousand photographs.

Nobody is at either extreme, but the gap explains almost every case of an account filling faster than expected. If your library looks modest and your storage keeps disappearing, video is the reason, essentially always.

Frame rate matters as much as resolution. 4K at 60 frames per second is roughly double the size of 4K at 30, for footage that looks near identical on a phone. A lot of phones ship with the higher setting on by default, so plenty of people are recording at twice the file size without ever having chosen to.

If you record video regularly and want cloud storage for large files to stretch further, the single highest-value change is dropping everyday clips to 1080p and reserving 4K for things you intend to keep and edit. That alone can quadruple how long an allowance lasts.

Documents are effectively free

A scanned receipt is a few hundred kilobytes. A long PDF is perhaps two or three megabytes. A spreadsheet or a text document is smaller still.

You could put every piece of paper in your house into free file storage online and barely register it against 100GB. A thousand scanned documents at 500KB each comes to half a gigabyte, which is one half of one percent of your allowance.

People often hesitate over adding documents to save space, while a single afternoon of 4K video uses fifty times as much. The instinct is backwards. Add the documents. They are the files that exist in only one place, and they cost nothing.

Music and audio sit in between. A compressed track is three to eight megabytes, so a substantial music library runs to a few gigabytes. Voice memos and recorded interviews are smaller than people expect, usually around a megabyte per minute.

Free cloud storage for photos: what to watch for

Photos are the reason most people go looking for storage in the first place, and there are two decisions that matter more than the size of the allowance.

The first is whether originals are kept. Some services offer a larger free tier by storing a resized version of every image. On a phone screen you will never notice. You notice the first time you print something, or crop into a photo to get the detail you remember being there, and find it has been thrown away. That decision is made once, at upload, and cannot be undone later. An allowance that sounds smaller but keeps originals is usually the better deal.

The second is coverage. A photo backup that only watches your camera folder will miss a surprising amount: pictures people sent you in messaging apps, screenshots you meant to keep, images saved from a browser, and on Android, edits that some gallery apps write to a separate folder. People discover this months later while looking for something specific, which is the worst possible moment.

Free photo cloud storage is worth judging on those two things before capacity. 100GB of full-quality photos with every folder covered is worth more than a larger allowance holding compressed copies of your camera roll only.

One more consideration if you are moving a library from somewhere else. Check that you can get files out as easily as you put them in. Being able to download your own photos in bulk, in their original format, is what stops storage from becoming a place your files go and do not come back from.

Free cloud storage for videos and large files

Video is where an allowance is won or lost, so it is worth understanding what drives file size before assuming you need a bigger plan.

Three settings control almost all of it, and most phones ship with at least one of them turned up higher than the owner would choose.

Resolution. 4K is roughly four times the data of 1080p for the same length of footage. For clips you will watch on a phone, the difference is close to invisible.

Frame rate. 60fps is double the data of 30fps. Useful for fast motion you intend to slow down, unnecessary for a birthday party.

Length. The one nobody adjusts. Most phone video is far longer than anyone rewatches. Trimming a clip before it uploads is the quickest way to reclaim space.

Combined, dropping from 4K at 60fps to 1080p at 30fps reduces a file to something like an eighth of its original size. That turns five hours of headroom into forty, without buying anything.

For large individual files, an hour-long recording or a big archive, cloud storage for large files works exactly like anything else. There is no separate mechanism. It takes longer to move and uses more of your allowance while it sits there. Run those uploads on Wi-Fi and on power, and be patient with the first one, because most home connections upload at a small fraction of their download speed.

The habit that makes free video cloud storage workable long term is archiving rather than accumulating. Footage you have already edited, shared or watched once does not need to stay live alongside this month's material. Deciding that periodically is less painful than being forced into it when an account fills.

Free online file backup is not the same as sync

These get used interchangeably and they behave very differently at the moment you need them, which is a bad time to discover the distinction.

Sync keeps the same set of files on several devices. Change something in one place and the change appears everywhere. That is the point, and it is useful. It also means a deletion propagates just as faithfully as an edit. Delete a folder on your laptop and a well-behaved sync service will dutifully remove it from your phone as well, because that is what you appeared to ask for.

Backup keeps a copy somewhere that is not affected by what happens to the original. If the phone is lost, the copy is unaffected. If you delete something locally, the copy is still there until you go and remove it deliberately.

Most people want backup and have been sold sync. The symptom is the story that comes up constantly: someone tidied up their phone, or a family member did, and the photos vanished from every device at once. Nothing malfunctioned. Sync did exactly what sync does.

Free online file backup is worth having alongside whatever sync you already use, rather than instead of it. They solve different problems and neither is a substitute for the other.

The practical test: if deleting a file on your phone makes it disappear everywhere, that service is syncing, not backing up. That is fine, as long as you know which one you have.

Making 100GB last as long as possible

None of these require paying for anything, and together they can easily double or triple how long an allowance lasts.

  1. Drop everyday video to 1080p. The single biggest change available to you. Keep 4K for things you intend to edit or keep permanently.
  2. Turn off 60fps unless you need it. Halves the size of everything you record, for footage most people cannot distinguish.
  3. Exclude folders that do not matter. Screenshots of memes, downloaded images, app caches. Over a year these add up to real volume with no sentimental value.
  4. Delete burst sequences after picking the keeper. Fifteen near-identical frames of the same moment is fifteen times the storage of one good photo.
  5. Trim long clips before uploading. The first forty seconds of pointing the camera at the floor does not need archiving.
  6. Do an annual pass on old video. Not photos, video. Ten minutes of deleting finished footage frees more than an afternoon of deleting pictures.

What not to do: do not turn off backup for folders you care about in order to save room. Running out of space is an inconvenience. Discovering a folder was excluded for two years is not.

Equally, do not spend hours curating before you have backed anything up. Get everything protected first, then optimise. A complete backup you tidy later beats a perfectly organised one you never finished.

Who 100GB is enough for

Rather than generalise, here are the situations where a free plan is comfortably sufficient and the ones where it is not.

Comfortably enough: someone with a phone or two who mostly photographs family, holidays and daily life, records short clips occasionally, and keeps documents and receipts. This describes the majority of phone owners and 100GB will run for years with room left over.

Enough with a little attention: someone who records video regularly but in short bursts, or who has a large existing library of several years to bring across at once. The initial upload uses a big chunk and then growth is manageable. Occasional archiving keeps it working.

Not enough for long: anyone recording long 4K video routinely, anyone working with camera files alongside phone photos, and households running several heavy camera users through one account. In these cases 100GB is a good place to start and a larger plan is the honest answer within a year.

The reason this is worth thinking about at the beginning rather than at the limit is that a backup which stops working because an account is full is not much of a backup. Knowing roughly which category you fall into means you can plan for it rather than being interrupted by it.

And if you are not sure, start on the free plan and look at the number after a couple of months. Your own usage figure answers the question better than any estimate on a web page.

Will 100GB last you years or months?

You can answer this in about a minute with a number your phone already knows.

On iPhone, Settings, then General, then iPhone Storage. On Android, Settings, then Storage. Both will tell you what photos and videos are currently using.

That figure represents everything you have accumulated on this device so far. If your phone is two years old and Photos is using 40GB, you are generating roughly 20GB a year, and 100GB free cloud storage gives you about five years from a standing start, or three more years on top of what you already have.

Three broad profiles cover most people:

Mostly photos, occasional short clips. Perhaps 10GB to 20GB a year. 100GB lasts five years or more. This is the majority of phone users and free online storage at this size is generous for them.

Regular video, a few minutes at a time. Perhaps 30GB to 50GB a year. 100GB lasts two to three years. Comfortable, with an eye on it.

Heavy 4K video, long recordings. 100GB or more a year. You will fill it within a year, and a larger plan or a deliberate archiving habit is the honest answer.

Households are the other variable. Several phones backing into one account share the allowance rather than each getting their own, so a family of four is a different calculation from one person.

What free means here

There are several different things the word free can mean in this market, and they are worth telling apart before you upload a library into any of them.

The free 100GB cloud storage on this free cloud storage app is included with every account. No card at signup, and no trial window that expires and takes your allowance with it. If you never pay anything, you keep 100GB.

What to check when comparing anything described as free online storage:

  1. Is it permanent or a trial? A large allowance for thirty days is a paid product with a delay. This is the most common thing dressed up as free.
  2. Does it need a card to start? Requiring payment details for a free tier usually means it converts to paid unless you intervene.
  3. What happens at the limit? Uploads pausing is reasonable behaviour. Existing files being deleted is not.
  4. Are the originals kept, or compressed copies? Some services offer more room by reducing quality. That is a real cost, just not a financial one.
  5. Is the limit stated plainly? Storage costs money to provide. An allowance described in vague terms rather than a number usually has a limit written somewhere less prominent.

Cloud storage without paying is a reasonable thing to want and a reasonable thing to get. Find out which version of free you have been offered.

Putting the first 100GB to use

An allowance sitting empty protects nothing. The gap between signing up and having a backup you can rely on is where most people stall.

The first upload is the only demanding part. A phone holding several years of photos is tens of gigabytes moving over a connection that was designed to download rather than upload, so it takes hours and sometimes runs overnight. That is normal and it is not a sign anything is wrong.

Three things make it painless:

  1. Start it in the evening, on Wi-Fi, plugged in. You are not required to watch. Both Android and iOS will throttle a long upload once the battery drops, so power matters more than people expect.
  2. Do not tidy first. The instinct is to sort through everything before uploading. It delays the backup by weeks and the space you save is trivial. Upload everything, curate later if you want to.
  3. Check it in the morning from a browser. Not from the phone. Open your account on a computer and confirm a recent photo opens. That single check is what turns an assumption into a backup.

After the first run, everything else is incremental. New photos join a queue and go up in the background, and daily volumes are small enough that you stop noticing it happens.

If your library is larger than 100GB to begin with, upload in order of importance rather than waiting until you can do all of it. Camera roll first, then documents, then the rest. A partial backup that exists is worth far more than a complete one you never started.

Five mistakes that waste an allowance

Backing up app caches and downloads. Some setups will happily upload cached images, downloaded media and temporary files. None of it is worth keeping and collectively it can be gigabytes. Check what folders are included rather than accepting everything.

Keeping every burst and every duplicate. Phones make it very easy to take fifteen versions of one moment. Uploading all fifteen is fifteen times the storage for one photograph you will look at.

Recording in 4K by default. Not a decision most people made, just the setting the phone arrived with. It quadruples video size against 1080p for footage watched on a phone screen.

Treating the account as a filing cabinet with no lid. Uploading is easy and reviewing never happens. An annual pass over old video takes ten minutes and reclaims more than any other single action.

Running several phones through one account without planning for it. The allowance is shared, not multiplied. Two heavy camera users in a household will reach the limit roughly twice as fast as one.

None of these are catastrophic and all of them are easy to correct once you know they are happening. The common thread is that they are defaults rather than choices, which is exactly why they go unnoticed.

What happens when you fill it

Uploads pause. Nothing already backed up is deleted for reaching the limit.

Some services behave differently. Here, your files stay where they are. New uploads wait until there is room.

You then have three options, and they are not mutually exclusive.

Free up space in the account. Sort by size and look at video first. Ten large clips will free more room than a thousand photos. Old footage you have already edited and shared rarely needs to stay live.

Reduce what you are adding. Dropping everyday recording from 4K to 1080p cuts video size by roughly three quarters. Turning off backup for folders that do not matter, screenshots of memes for instance, removes a surprising volume over a year.

Gigabytes, terabytes and why the numbers never quite match

A quick detour that saves confusion later, because the figure your phone reports and the figure on a storage plan are measured slightly differently.

A terabyte is a thousand gigabytes, so the 1TB plan is ten times the free 100GB, and 250GB and 500GB sit at two and a half and five times it respectively. That much is straightforward.

Where it gets muddy is that storage is sometimes counted in powers of ten and sometimes in powers of two. A drive sold as 128GB shows up as around 119GB once a phone has formatted it and reserved space for the operating system. Nothing has been taken from you, the two systems count differently, and the gap widens as the numbers grow.

The practical effect is that you should not expect the number on your phone's storage screen to match a plan size exactly, and a shortfall of a few percent is normal rather than a sign something is wrong.

For estimating purposes none of this matters much. The rule of thumb of roughly 300 photos per gigabyte already carries more uncertainty than the counting convention does, because the size of a photo varies far more than a few percent depending on what is in it. A picture of a blank wall compresses to almost nothing. A detailed landscape at the same resolution can be several times larger.

That variability is worth remembering whenever you see a precise-sounding claim about how many photos an allowance holds. The honest version is always a range.

How 100GB compares to what you already have

Most people already have some free online storage attached to an account they did not choose deliberately, and it helps to know where 100GB sits against it.

Apple gives every iCloud account 5GB. That is enough for device settings and messages and nowhere near enough for a photo library, which is why the storage full notification is such a familiar part of owning an iPhone. 100GB is twenty times that.

Typical free tiers elsewhere run from a couple of gigabytes to around fifteen, often shared across mail, documents and photos at the same time, so the effective figure for photos alone is lower than the headline.

The practical difference is what a tier lets you do. Anything under about 20GB means making decisions about what to keep, which in practice means people stop using it. 100GB is enough to back up a whole phone without curating first, and that is the threshold where a backup becomes something you set up once instead of something you manage.

It is also worth separating storage from sync. Services that keep the same library on several devices are doing something different from keeping a second copy safe. A deletion that syncs everywhere is correct sync behaviour and useless as a backup.
